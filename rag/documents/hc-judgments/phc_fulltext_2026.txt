J U D G M E N T
SYED  MUDASSER  AMEER,  J.  Through  this  Regular  First
Appeal No.127 of 2026, the appellant/plaintiff,  Fiber Pak Kids
Planet through partner Umar Hayat , has called in question the
judgment and preliminary decree dated 25.02.2026 passed by the
learned Additional District Judge-VII, Haripur, in Suit No.61/1 of
2019,  whereby  the  suit  for  declaration,  rendition  of  accounts,
specific performance and dissolution of partnership was partially
decreed. The appellant has also challenged the decree to the extent
that the learned trial Court failed to specify, with requisite clarity,
the  accounts,  assets,  liabilities,  businesses  and  transactions  in
respect whereof his 40% share was to be determined. Regular First
Appeal  No.201  of  2026  has,  conversely,  been  filed  by  the
defendants, namely Azeemullah and others, assailing the same
judgment and preliminary decree, principally contending that the
partnership stood dissolved upon the notice dated 18.08.2017 and
that the plaintiff was not entitled to any share in the subsequent
business or assets of the defendants. Since both appeals arise out of
the same judgment, involve common questions of fact and law and
are directed against the same preliminary decree, they are being
decided through this consolidated judgment.
2.  Briefly stated, the plaintiff and defendants No.1 to 3 were
partners in a registered firm namely  Fiber Pak Kids Planet ,
constituted under partnership deed dated 20.08.2015. The plaintiff
held 40% share, defendant No.1 Azeemullah also held 40% share,
while defendants No.2 and 3 held 10% each. The partnership deed
contemplated the business of the firm to be carried on at Naseem
Town, Swat Chowk, Haripur, and contained, inter alia, provisions
relating to the nature and duration of the partnership, retirement of
a partner, distribution of assets and settlement of accounts. The
deed also provided in clause 10 that a partner intending to retire
was to give one month's notice in writing, whereupon the amount
of his share was to be allocated according to the prevailing market
value. The deed, however, expressly provided in clause 1 that the
partnership was  at will . The original Form-H produced from the
office of the Registrar of Firms also records the four partners and
their respective shares.
3.  It is not disputed that on 18.08.2017, defendants No.1 to
3 addressed a written notice to the plaintiff. Though the document
bears the heading indicating  “Cancellation of Partnership” , it
also refers to clause 10 of the partnership deed, dealing with
retirement of a partner. The plaintiff's case is that the notice,
properly construed, was not a valid dissolution notice and that the
partnership continued thereafter. His further case is that, after the
notice, attempts were made through Jirgas to settle the affairs of
the firm and determine the mode and manner of its dissolution,
but the process ultimately ended without a binding settlement.
According to him, instead of settling the accounts and distributing
the  assets,  defendants  No.1  to  4  took  over  the  business,
machinery, stock, records, bank accounts, goodwill, telephones,
email address and other business infrastructure of Fiber Pak Kids
Planet and thereafter commenced or continued their business
through  Azeem Industries ,  Azeem Play Land Industries LLP
and other concerns, thereby excluding the plaintiff from his 40%
interest. The record contains material relating to the subsequent
concerns and also reflects that the original firm's official address,
telephone,  email  and  other  business  identifiers  continued  to
feature in the later business activities.
4.  The defendants, on the other hand, maintain that the
partnership being one at will, the notice dated 18.08.2017 validly
dissolved the firm in terms of section 43 of the Partnership Act,
1932. Their contention is that after such dissolution they were free
to carry on their own business and that the subsequent concerns
constituted independent entities. They also dispute the plaintiff's
claim regarding the ownership of the assets and, particularly, the
status  of  Plot  No.92-A,  Zone-B,  Phase-VII,  Hattar  Industrial
Estate, asserting that the same was allotted in the individual name
of Azeemullah and did not become partnership property merely
because the firm's business was carried on there. The trial Court
accepted the plaintiff's entitlement to a 40% share and directed
settlement of accounts, but did not finally and distinctly determine
the legal effect of the notice dated 18.08.2017 or specify with
sufficient precision the corpus, period and methodology of the
accounting exercise.
5.  Learned counsel for the parties were heard in detail and
the  entire  record,  including  the  pleadings,  partnership  deed,
documentary  evidence,  statements  of  the  witnesses  and  the
material relating to the subsequent concerns, was examined.
6.  The  first  and  foundational  question  is  whether  the
partnership stood dissolved upon the notice dated 18.08.2017. The
answer cannot be determined merely by looking at the caption of
the document or, conversely, by fastening upon its reference to
clause 10. The notice has to be construed as a whole, keeping in
view the express terms of the partnership deed and the statutory
scheme. Clause 1 of the partnership deed admittedly declares the
partnership to be  at will , and section 43(1) of the Partnership Act,
1932 provides that, where a partnership is at will, the firm may be
dissolved by any partner giving notice in writing to all the other
partners of his intention to dissolve the firm; subsection (2) further
provides that the dissolution takes effect from the date mentioned
in the notice or, where no date is mentioned, from the date of
communication of the notice.
7.  There  is,  at  the  same  time,  a  clear  legal  distinction
between  retirement of a partner  and  dissolution of the firm .
Upon retirement, the partnership between the remaining partners
may continue in a reconstituted form, whereas upon dissolution
the affairs of the firm have to be wound up and the accounts
settled. This distinction has been recognized in  M. Yousuf Adil
Saleem  and  Company  (2007  PTD  1636)  and  has  also  been
reaffirmed in the jurisprudence concerning sections 37 and 48 of
the Partnership Act. Of particular relevance is  Nadir Khan v.
Qadir  Hussain  and  others  (2024  SCMR  770),  wherein  the
Hon'ble Supreme Court emphasized the statutory requirements
governing retirement under section 32 and distinguished that
mode from dissolution.
8.  Applying  these  principles  to  the  present  case,  the
reference in the notice to clause 10 does create some ambiguity as
to the precise mode initially contemplated by the signatories.
Nevertheless, the document, when read as a whole, expressly
communicates an intention to bring the partnership itself to an
end. The description of the document as one for cancellation of
the partnership, the reference to cessation of the partnership
business and the fact that the firm was admittedly a partnership at
will collectively demonstrate an intention to dissolve the firm
rather than merely to retire one partner and continue the same
partnership  with  the  remaining  partners.  A  mistaken  or
superfluous reference to clause 10 cannot, by itself, defeat the
operative intention conveyed by the notice. We, therefore, hold
that the notice dated 18.08.2017 constituted a notice of dissolution
under section 43 of the Partnership Act and that the partnership
stood dissolved with effect from  18.09.2017 , i.e. on expiry of the
period mentioned in the notice.
9.  The existence of subsequent Jirga proceedings does not
lead to a contrary conclusion. Those proceedings undoubtedly
demonstrate that the parties were attempting to work out their
differences and settle the affairs of the business, but they do not,
in the absence of a concluded and mutually binding agreement,
obliterate the statutory effect of the notice or revive a partnership
already dissolved under section 43. The ultimate termination of
the Jirga proceedings, as borne out from the record, therefore has
no independent effect upon the date of dissolution. The Jirga
material is, however, of considerable evidentiary significance for
another reason: it sheds light upon the fact that, notwithstanding
the notice, the affairs, assets and accounts of the dissolved firm
were not finally settled between the parties.
10.  Nor does the continued appearance of the firm in the
records of the Registrar of Firms by itself establish that the
partnership remained legally subsisting. The Form-H produced by
the plaintiff records the original constitution and shares of the
partners, while the subsequent status material from the Registrar
reflects absence of a recorded proceeding regarding reconstitution
or dissolution at the relevant time. Such  material  may have
corroborative value regarding the conduct of the parties and the
public record, but it cannot override a dissolution validly effected
inter se under section 43 of the Act. The statutory consequence of
a valid notice cannot be defeated merely by an omission or delay
in updating the record of the Registrar.
11.  The  conclusion  that  the  firm  stood  dissolved  on
18.09.2017, however, does  not  dispose of the plaintiff's claim.
This is the point on which the principal error in the defendants'
case lies. Dissolution is not synonymous with extinction of the
proprietary and financial rights of the partners. Section 46 of the
Partnership Act expressly entitles every partner, upon dissolution,
to have the property of the firm applied towards its debts and
liabilities  and  the  surplus  distributed  amongst  the  partners
according to their respective rights. Section 48 prescribes the
mode in which the accounts of a dissolved firm are to be settled.
12.  More significantly, section 37 specifically protects an
outgoing partner where the surviving or continuing partners carry
on the business of the firm with  the property of the firm without
final settlement of accounts . In such circumstances, the outgoing
partner is entitled, at his option, to such share of the profits made
since he ceased to be a partner as may be attributable to the use of
his share of the firm's property, or to interest at six per cent per
annum on the amount of his share in the property of the firm. The
Supreme Court of Pakistan, in  Shamsuddin v. Imamuddin  (1992
SCMR 1239), considered the scope of section 37 in relation to
business carried on after dissolution and recognized the relevance
of the statutory right of an outgoing partner where the business
continued to be carried on with the property of the firm without
final settlement of accounts.
13.  The  legal  position  is  thus  unmistakable:  dissolution
brings the partnership to an end; it does not authorize one group of
partners to appropriate the firm's assets and continue its business
for their exclusive benefit without first settling the outgoing
partner's entitlement. The purpose of dissolution is to bring the
joint business affairs to a close through winding up, adjustment of
liabilities, realization and valuation of assets and distribution of
the surplus. Sections 46 to 48 embody this statutory scheme.
14.  The evidence available on the present record raises a
substantial  and,  in  my  view,  genuine  question  whether  this
statutory  process  was  ever  completed.  The  record  contains
material suggesting that, after the notice, defendants No.1 to 4
took possession or control of the machinery, tools, office record,
cash, bank accounts and other assets of Fiber Pak Kids Planet and
thereafter operated through Azeem Industries and other concerns.
The  subsequent  proceedings  and  reports  also  record  the
controversy regarding the continued use of the old firm's business
infrastructure  and  assets  by  the  subsequent  concerns.  The
evidence reproduced in the appeal record further shows that the
respondents were confronted in cross-examination with the use of
the  original  firm's  telephone  number,  email  address,  office
particulars and other business identifiers by the later concerns, as
well as with the continued operation of the original firm's bank
accounts.
15.  These circumstances cannot be brushed aside by merely
saying that a new partnership deed or a new corporate name was
subsequently adopted. A change in the name or legal form of a
business does not, by itself, determine the ownership of the assets
used by it. The decisive question is whether the assets, stock,
machinery,  goodwill,  business  connection,  receivables,  cash,
records, premises or other property employed in the subsequent
business belonged to the original firm, and if so, whether the
plaintiff's 40% interest therein was ever settled. The present
record does not permit that question to be answered merely on the
competing assertions of the parties.
16.  The contention of the plaintiff that he is automatically
entitled to 40% of the entire profits of Azeem Industries, Azeem
Play  Land  Industries  LLP  and  every  subsequent  concern  is,
equally, too broad. His entitlement under the Partnership Act is
not a perpetual proprietary interest in every subsequent enterprise
established by his former partners. What requires ascertainment is
the extent to which the subsequent business was carried on by
employing, utilizing, transferring or otherwise benefiting from  the
property or business of Fiber Pak Kids Planet  without final
settlement  of  his  share.  Where  such  use  is  established,  the
statutory consequences under section 37, read with sections 46
and 48, may follow. Where, on the other hand, an asset or capital
investment is proved to have been independently acquired after
dissolution from the respondents' own resources and without
employment of the property or business connection of the original
firm, the same cannot simply be brought into the plaintiff's 40%
share.
17.  The same principle applies to the claim concerning  Plot
No.92-A,  Zone-B, Phase-VII, Hattar Industrial Estate. The mere
fact that the original firm's business was conducted there does not,
without more, make the title to the plot partnership property. The
documentary material shows that the allotment/lease stood in the
individual name of Azeemullah, and the trial Court was therefore
justified in not automatically treating the title to the land itself as
an asset of the partnership. The question whether any partnership
funds were utilized for acquisition, construction, development or
improvement  of  the  premises,  and  whether  any  legally
recognizable interest of the firm arose therefrom, is nevertheless a
matter capable of verification in the accounting exercise.
18.  I am also not persuaded by the defendants' contention that
the plaintiff's suit must fail merely because he did not, at the
institution  of  the  suit,  furnish  an  exhaustive  item-by-item
inventory  of  every  asset  and  transaction.  A  partner  seeking
rendition of accounts cannot reasonably be required to prove in
advance  the  very  accounts  and  transactions  which  are
substantially within the control or knowledge of the managing
partners. In a partnership accounting suit, what is essential is
proof of the partnership, its constitution and shares, and the
existence of an entitlement to accounts; the exact extent of the
assets,  liabilities,  transactions  and  profits  is  thereafter  to  be
ascertained through the accounting process. The jurisprudence
concerning rendition of partnership accounts is consistent on this
point.  The  courts  have  also  sanctioned  appointment  of  a
competent person where the managing partners failed to produce
the books, accounts and business records necessary for settlement.
The decision is  Abdul Ghani and others v. Abdul Rashid and
others  (PLJ 2009 Karachi 20) is illustrative, where failure of
managing partners to produce the accounts of the partnership
resulted in directions for taking over and examining the firm's
assets and records for preparation of final accounts.
19.  The preliminary decree passed by the learned trial Court,
therefore, cannot be sustained in its present form. It correctly
recognized the plaintiff's entitlement to his 40% share, but it did
not define with sufficient precision the accounting period, the
assets and liabilities to be taken into account, the subsequent
concerns to be examined, the consequences of utilization of the
original firm's assets, or the methodology by which the plaintiff's
eventual entitlement is to be calculated. The resultant uncertainty
is capable of generating further litigation at the stage of final
decree and, therefore, requires correction at this stage.
20.  The appropriate course is not, however, to dismiss the
suit as urged by the defendants. Nor is it to accept the plaintiff's
contention that every subsequent enterprise of the defendants
forms  part  of  the  original  partnership.  The  decree  requires
structured modification so that the parties' rights may be finally
determined on the basis of a transparent accounting process.
21.  Accordingly,  the  following  specific  findings  and
directions shall form part of the preliminary decree:
(i)  Fiber Pak Kids Planet was a partnership at will under
clause 1 of the partnership deed dated 20.08.2015.
(ii)  The  notice  dated  18.08.2017,  read  as  a  whole,
constituted a notice of dissolution under section 43 of
the  Partnership  Act,  1932,  notwithstanding  its
reference to clause 10 relating to retirement.
(iii)  The original partnership stood dissolved with effect
from 18.09.2017.
(iv)  The Jirga proceedings conducted thereafter did not, in
the absence of a concluded and binding agreement,
revive the dissolved partnership or alter the statutory
date of dissolution; however, the said proceedings and
the subsequent conduct of the parties are relevant to
the determination of whether the affairs and assets of
the dissolved firm were ever finally settled.
(v)  The plaintiff Umar Hayat is entitled to settlement of
his  40%  share  in  the  property,  assets,  liabilities,
profits and other financial interests of Fiber Pak Kids
Planet,  subject  to  the  partnership  deed  and  the
Partnership Act, 1932.
(vi)  The accounting shall extend from the date relevant
under the partnership deed and the evidence to the
date of dissolution, and thereafter, insofar as required
by sections 37, 46 and 48 of the Act, until the date of
actual final settlement of the plaintiff's share.
(vii)  The accounting shall determine,  inter alia , all cash in
hand, bank balances, receivables, liabilities, stock-in-
trade,  machinery,  tools,  equipment,  office  assets,
vehicles,  business  records,  goodwill,  business
connections and all other property belonging to Fiber
Pak Kids Planet as on the date of dissolution.
(viii)  It shall further determine whether any of the aforesaid
assets  were  thereafter  transferred,  appropriated,
utilized or employed in Azeem Industries, Azeem
Play  Land  Industries  LLP,  or  any  other  concern,
business  or  enterprise  established,  operated  or
controlled by defendants No.1 to 4 or any of them.
(ix)  Where any property or business connection of Fiber
Pak  Kids  Planet  is  found  to  have  been  used  in
carrying  on  a  subsequent  business  without  final
settlement  of  the  plaintiff's  share,  the  profits
attributable  to  such  use  shall  be  determined  in
accordance with section 37 of the Partnership Act, or,
where applicable, the statutory alternative available to
the outgoing partner, after taking into account the
terms  of  the  partnership  deed  and  all  relevant
liabilities.
(x)  Any asset proved to have been independently acquired
by the defendants after dissolution from their own
funds, and not out of or through the property, assets,
goodwill or business connection of Fiber Pak Kids
Planet, shall not be treated as an asset of the original
partnership merely because it was used in a business
of the same or similar nature.
(xi)  The ownership/title of Plot No.92-A, Zone-B, Phase-
VII, Hattar Industrial Estate shall not be treated as
belonging to the partnership merely on account of the
firm's  business  having  been  conducted  there.  The
audit/accounting exercise shall, however, determine
whether  any  partnership  funds  were  utilized  in
acquisition,  construction,  development  or
improvement  of  the  premises  and  the  legal  and
financial consequences thereof.
(xii)  All bank accounts operated for the original firm, all
books of account, ledgers, journals, vouchers, stock
registers,  invoices,  receipts,  tax  records,
correspondence, business records, electronic records
and other material relevant to the affairs of Fiber Pak
Kids Planet, as well as records of the subsequent
concerns  insofar  as  they  relate  to  the  alleged
utilization of the original firm's assets or business,
shall be brought within the accounting exercise.
22.  For an effective and impartial determination of these
matters, the learned trial Court shall, forthwith after receipt of a
certified copy of this judgment, appoint a reputed Chartered
Accountant  or  a  well-established  Audit  Firm  of  recognized
professional  standing,  preferably  one  having  experience  in
forensic accounting and partnership/business disputes, to conduct
a forensic audit and complete financial examination of the affairs
of Fiber Pak Kids Planet and, to the extent relevant for tracing the
assets and business of the original firm, of Azeem Industries,
Azeem  Play  Land  Industries  LLP  and  any  other  subsequent
concern found prima facie connected with the assets, business,
transactions or goodwill of Fiber Pak Kids Planet.
23.  The appointed Chartered Accountant/Audit Firm shall be
empowered, subject to the supervision of the learned trial Court,
to call for and examine all relevant books of account, bank
statements, tax returns, stock registers, invoices, purchase and sale
records,  machinery  registers,  fixed-asset  registers,  receipts,
vouchers,  electronic  records,  official  registrations,  partnership
deeds, balance sheets, income tax and sales tax material and any
other  record  necessary  to  trace  the  origin,  movement  and
utilization of the assets and funds of the original partnership. The
parties shall extend full cooperation and shall produce all records
within their custody, possession or control. Where any record is
withheld without lawful justification, the learned trial Court shall
be at liberty to draw such inference as may be permissible in law.
24.  The  audit  report  shall  specifically  identify  and
distinguish:
(a)  assets  of  Fiber  Pak  Kids  Planet  existing  on
18.09.2017;
(b)  liabilities of the firm on that date;
(c)  the  assets  thereafter  transferred,  removed,  sold,
disposed  of,  appropriated  or  utilized  by  the
defendants;
(d)  the present assets of Azeem Industries, Azeem Play
Land Industries LLP or any other subsequent concern
which are traceable to, represent, replace or have been
acquired out of the assets, funds, stock, receivables or
other property of Fiber Pak Kids Planet;
(e)  the proceeds realized from any sale or disposal of the
assets of the erstwhile partnership and the manner in
which such proceeds were utilized;
(f)  the profits attributable to the use of the property,
assets or business connection of Fiber Pak Kids Planet
after its dissolution; and
(g)  the  amount  ultimately  payable  to  the  plaintiff
representing his 40% entitlement after adjustment of
all lawful liabilities, credits and other permissible
adjustments.
The report shall be accompanied by supporting schedules and
source documents so that each material conclusion is capable of
verification by the parties and the Court.
25.  The learned trial Court shall thereafter afford both sides
full opportunity to file objections to the forensic audit report and,
where necessary, summon the Chartered Accountant/Audit Firm
for clarification. Upon consideration of the report, the objections
and the evidence already available on the record, the learned trial
Court  shall  proceed  to  pass  the  appropriate  final  decree  in
accordance with sections 37, 46 and 48 of the Partnership Act and
the findings recorded herein.
26.  Since the final determination of the plaintiff's entitlement
depends upon identification, preservation and tracing of the assets,
records and business interests of the erstwhile partnership, it is
necessary to preserve the subject matter of the lis during the
pendency  of  the  accounting  proceedings.  Accordingly,  until
passing of the final decree or further orders of the learned trial
Court, the defendants shall not sell, transfer, alienate, mortgage,
pledge, encumber or otherwise dispose of any asset of Fiber Pak
Kids Planet existing as on 18.09.2017, or any asset specifically
identified during the forensic audit as having been transferred
from, acquired through or representing the property or funds of
the said partnership, without prior permission of the learned trial
Court. They shall also not destroy, conceal, remove, alter or
otherwise  tamper  with  any  books  of  account,  bank  record,
register, voucher, invoice, electronic record or other document
relevant to the affairs of Fiber Pak Kids Planet or the tracing of its
assets into any subsequent concern.
27.  The  aforesaid  direction  shall  not  be  construed  as
restraining the defendants from carrying on their lawful business
in the ordinary course or from dealing with assets independently
acquired by them after dissolution, subject to their producing the
relevant record before the learned trial Court/appointed auditor
and subject to any further order passed upon identification of the
assets belonging to, or derived from, the erstwhile partnership.
28.  Within  fifteen  days  of  receipt  of  this  judgment,
defendants No.1 to 4 shall place before the learned trial Court a
complete  inventory  of  the  movable  and  immovable  assets,
machinery, stock, bank accounts, receivables and other property
of Fiber Pak Kids Planet which came into their possession or
control upon or after its dissolution, along with particulars of their
present location and disposition, and shall separately disclose the
assets claimed by them to have been independently acquired
thereafter. The appointed Chartered Accountant/Audit Firm shall
verify these disclosures against the available documentary and
financial record.
29.  In this view of the matter, the grievance of the defendants
that the preliminary decree was insufficiently specific is partly
well-founded,  but  the  consequence  sought  by  them,  namely
dismissal of the plaintiff's suit, does not follow. Likewise, the
plaintiff's grievance that the decree did not sufficiently identify
the assets and accounts requiring determination is also justified.
The  solution  lies  in  modification  and  elaboration  of  the
preliminary  decree ,  rather  than  reversal  of  the  plaintiff's
substantive entitlement.
30.  So far as the defendants' counterclaim of Rs.3,000,000/-
is concerned, the same shall be examined by the learned trial
Court in accordance with law, independently of the determination
of  the  partnership  accounts,  after  evaluating  the  alleged
settlement, cheque and the Jirga proceedings on the basis of
admissible evidence. No observation contained herein shall be
construed as a final determination of that claim.
31.  For the foregoing reasons,  RFA No.127 of 2026 and
RFA No.201 of 2026 are both disposed of with modification  of
the judgment and preliminary decree dated 25.02.2026 passed by
the  learned  Additional  District  Judge-VII,  Haripur,  in  Suit
No.61/1 of 2019, in the manner and to the extent indicated
hereinabove. The plaintiff's entitlement to settlement of his 40%
share in the affairs of Fiber Pak Kids Planet is maintained;
however, his entitlement in respect of subsequent businesses shall
be confined to such property, assets, business connection and
profits as are found, upon proper accounting, to be attributable to
the original partnership in accordance with law. The learned trial
Court shall proceed with the matter expeditiously and pass final
decree  after  completion  of  the  aforesaid  forensic  audit  and
accounting exercise.
32.  The  observations  made  herein  are  confined  to  the
determination of the appeals and shall not preclude the learned
trial Court from considering any matter falling within the scope of
the accounting and final decree strictly in accordance with the
evidence, the Partnership Act, 1932 and the findings recorded
above.

JUDGMENT SHEET
PESHAWAR HIGH COURT, PESHAWAR
( Judicial Department)
C.R No. 35-P of 2013 .
Present:
Mr. Abdul Samad Khan, Advocate, for petitioners.

JUDGMENT
MUHAMMAD  NAEEM  ANWAR,  J.-  Impugned  through  the
instant Civil Revision Petition, filed under Section 115 of the Code of
Civil Procedure, 1908, is the judgment and decree dated 11.10.2012
passed  by  the  learned  Additional  District  Judge-VI,  Peshawar,
whereby the appeal preferred by the respondents/plaintiffs against
the judgment and decree dated 22.05.2009 passed by the learned
Civil Judge-X, Peshawar, dismissing their suit, was allowed to the
limited extent of cancellation of the tatimma prepared in respect of
the disputed property and the consequential allocation of a specific
portion thereof, while the alienation made by defendant No.1 in
favour of defendant No.5 was maintained. The petitioners, who are
aggrieved by the aforesaid judgment to the extent of cancellation of
the tatimma, have invoked the revisional jurisdiction of this Court.
2.  The facts giving rise to the present petition, in brief,
are that the respondents/plaintiffs instituted a suit for declaration,
asserting that defendants No.1 to 4, including the present petitioners,
were owners in possession of property comprised in Khasra No. 489
measuring 12 kanals 8 marlas, Khasra No. 310 measuring 8 kanals 7
marlas and Khasra No. 525 measuring 48 kanals 6 marlas, along with
other properties situated in Revenue Estate Chughulpura, Tehsil and
District Peshawar. There had been previous litigation between the
parties concerning inheritance flowing from Mst. Sahib Jan Sultan,
which controversy was ultimately decided by the Hon’ble Supreme
Court through judgment dated 30.05.1990 passed in Civil Appeal
No.  35-P  of  1988.  The  plaintiffs  asserted  that  proceedings  for
Rizwanullah Khan and others Vs Muhammad Usman
Khan and others
partition of the joint property had been initiated by them in 1983 and
that  a  mode  of  partition  had  already  been  prepared,  with  the
proceedings having reached an advanced stage. During the pendency
of those proceedings, they came to know that defendant No.1 had
purportedly transferred an area measuring 16 kanals in favour of
Mst. Fahmida Begum, his wife, towards dower. It was further alleged
that, during the pendency of the partition proceedings, defendants
No.5 and 6, in collusion with officials of the Revenue Department,
commenced construction over the disputed property. An application
was accordingly moved before the Revenue Officer for restraining
such  construction,  and  the  construction  raised  thereon  was
subsequently  demolished  pursuant  to  an  order  passed  by  the
competent authority.
The plaintiffs specifically challenged the legality of
Mutation No. 857 dated 10.03.1979, contending that the same had
been attested in violation of the applicable land laws. Their principal
grievance was that, in the absence of a lawful partition, preparation
and carving out of a tatimma in respect of a specific portion of a
jointly  owned  survey  number  amounted,  in  substance,  to  an
unauthorized separation of a defined parcel from joint property,
thereby affecting the rights of the remaining co-sharers. It was further
asserted that defendant No.1, being entitled only to his share in the
joint estate, could not lawfully alienate 16 kanals as a specific and
separately identified portion of the joint property in a manner
prejudicial to the rights of the other co-owners. Having failed to
secure correction of the revenue entries from defendants No.1 and
5, the plaintiffs were constrained to institute the suit.
3.  The suit was contested by defendants No.1 and 5 on
various factual and legal grounds. Their case was that the property
had already been privately partitioned amongst the co-owners and
each of them was in possession of his respective share. It was,
therefore, maintained that the alienation reflected through Mutation
No. 857 was lawful and could not validly be questioned by the
plaintiffs. Defendant No.6 also filed a written statement. The parties
were thereafter directed to produce their evidence. Muhammad
Jamil, Patwari Halqa of Revenue Estate Chughulpura, appeared as a
witness and produced the relevant revenue record, including the
goshwara-e-Milkiyat. Muhammad Usman Khan appeared as attorney
for the plaintiffs, whereafter the plaintiffs closed their evidence. Dr.
Rizwanullah Khan appeared as attorney for defendants No.1 and 5,
who also closed their evidence. Upon conclusion of the trial, the
learned Trial Court, through judgment and decree dated 22.05.2009,
dismissed the suit. The plaintiffs preferred an appeal, which was
initially allowed through judgment dated 07.01.2010. The matter
thereafter came before this Court in Civil Revision No. 742-P of
2010. The revision petition was allowed, the judgment and decree of
the learned Trial Court were set aside and the matter was remanded
to the learned Additional District Judge-VI, Peshawar, for a fresh
decision  after  hearing  the  parties.  Upon  remand,  the  learned
appellate Court, through the impugned judgment dated 11.10.2012,
allowed the plaintiffs’ appeal to the limited extent of cancellation of
the tatimma, while maintaining the alienation made by defendant
No.1 in favour of defendant No.5. Hence, the present Civil Revision
Petition.
4.  Learned counsel for the petitioners, Mr. Abdus Samad
Khan, Advocate, contended that the learned appellate Court failed to
appreciate the distinction between the right of a co-sharer to alienate
his undivided share and the subsequent adjustment of such alienation
in partition proceedings. Placing reliance upon Muhammad Muzaffar
Khan v. Muhammad Yusuf Khan (PLD 1959 SC (Pak.) 9), Shah
Hussain v. Abdul Qayum and others (1984 SCMR 427), Mst. Saima
v. Paramount Spinning Mills Ltd. and others (2011 SCMR 1039)
and Town Committee, Jalalpur Pirwala through Chairman v. Malik
Mehr Bakhsh and another (2007 YLR 82), learned counsel argued
that the predecessor-in-interest of petitioners No.1 to 5 possessed
substantial ownership in the revenue estate and, therefore, his right to
alienate his share in favour of his wife could not be curtailed merely
because the property was jointly owned. It was further contended that
Mutation  No.  879  had  subsequently  been  corrected  through
Mutation No. 5675 dated 25.06.2010 and that the latter mutation was
itself assailable. Learned counsel, while referring to the statement of
PW-1 and Exhibit PW-1/X-1, argued that Habibullah Khan was
recorded as owner of 172 kanals 2 marlas, out of which 79 kanals 5
marlas had been alienated, leaving 92 kanals 17 marlas still standing
in his ownership. On this basis, it was argued that the alienation did
not  exceed  the  transferor’s  total  entitlement  in  the  estate  and,
consequently, could not be declared unlawful. The learned counsel
thus sought restoration of the judgment and decree of the learned
Trial Court.
5.  Learned counsel appearing for the respondents, Mr.
Arshad Jamal Qureshi and Mr. Salman Fayaz Mir, Advocates, whose
arguments were heard on 10.08.2026, confined their submissions to
the preparation and attestation of tatimma pursuant to Mutation No.
857 dated 10.03.1979. According to learned counsel, the learned
appellate Court had correctly found that the tatimma could not
lawfully have been carved out from the jointly owned property in the
absence of the consent of all the recorded co-owners or a lawful
partition through the competent Revenue authority.
6.  Arguments have been heard and the record perused.
7.  At the outset, the objection concerning Mutation No.
5675 dated 25.06.2010 may conveniently be dealt with. The said
mutation was founded upon the judgment of the learned Additional
District Judge, Peshawar, in Civil Appeal No. 10/13. That judgment,
however, had already been set aside by this Court in Civil Revision
No. 742-P of 2010 and the matter was remanded to the learned
appellate Court for a fresh decision. It is also significant that, even
when Civil Revision No. 742-P of 2010 was decided on 11.06.2012, a
copy of Mutation No. 5675 had not been brought on the record. In
any event, once the judgment which constituted the foundation of the
said mutation stood set aside and the learned appellate Court was
directed to reconsider the matter afresh, Mutation No. 5675 could
not confer any independent or enduring legal consequence. The
revenue record, therefore, necessarily has to conform to the final
adjudication of the rights of the parties.
8.  The controversy concerning the alienation made by
defendant No.1 in favour of defendant No.5 stands on a different
footing from the question of preparation of the tatimma. The learned
appellate Court consciously maintained the alienation, and that part
of the judgment was never challenged by the plaintiffs/respondents.
To that extent, therefore, the finding has attained finality. Even
otherwise, a co-owner cannot be deprived of his fundamental right to
alienate his own undivided share in joint property merely because the
property has not yet been partitioned. A co-sharer may transfer his
interest in the joint estate, but he cannot transfer a better title, a larger
proprietary interest, or a specific parcel in excess of his own lawful
entitlement. The controlling principle is that a co-sharer can transfer
no better title than he himself possesses. Consequently, the validity of
an alienation by a co-owner is necessarily subject to the upper limit of
the transferor’s total entitlement in the entire joint estate. If a co-
sharer is in possession of a particular khasra number or a defined
portion thereof in the column of cultivation, such possession does
not, by itself, transform his possessory arrangement into exclusive
proprietary title. His alienation of a specific parcel may, therefore,
operate  between  the  transferor  and  transferee,  but  its  ultimate
efficacy remains subject to the transferor’s proprietary share and the
result of lawful partition. This principle is well settled in Itebar Shah
and others v. Ahmad Shah and others (2001 CLC 1021) and Abdul
Hameed  through  legal  heirs  and  others  v.  Shamssuddin  and
others (PLD 2008 SC 140). The vendee steps into the shoes of the
vendor and acquires precisely the interest which the vendor could
lawfully convey—no more and no less.
9.  The  same  principle  was  lucidly  enunciated
in Muhammad Zafar Khan, wherein it was held that the vendee of a
co-sharer, who owns an undivided khata jointly with others, acquires
the same rights as his vendor. Even where the vendor is in exclusive
possession of a particular portion of the joint land and transfers
possession thereof, the vendee remains a co-sharer in the joint
property to the extent of the area purchased, provided that such area
does not exceed the share of the vendor in the entire joint estate.
The vendee may retain possession of the specific portion until
partition, but such possession remains subject to adjustment at the
time of partition. Thus, alienation of a specific portion by a co-sharer
is not, by itself, rendered void merely because the joint property has
not been partitioned; rather, the alienation remains subject to the
vendor’s  total  proprietary  entitlement  and  to  adjustment  in  the
eventual  partition. If  the  specific  area  transferred  exceeds  the
vendor’s share, the excess cannot be enforced against the other co-
sharers and has to be adjusted from the vendor’s remaining share in
the joint estate or otherwise dealt with in the partition proceedings.
This  principle  has  also  been  recognized  in Mst.  Saima (supra)
and Shah Hussain (supra).
10.  The  distinction  between  the validity  of  an
alienation and the validity of a tatimma is, however, decisive for the
present controversy. The learned counsel for the petitioners has
correctly relied upon the principle governing alienation by a co-
sharer, but that principle does not authorize a unilateral separation of
a specific parcel from a jointly owned survey number. A tatimma is
essentially a revenue device for identifying and preparing a sub-
division of an existing survey/khasra number. It is not, in itself, a
mode of partition whereby the proprietary rights of all co-sharers can
be separated or a defined parcel can be taken out of the joint
character of the property at the unilateral instance of one co-sharer.
The preparation of a tatimma, where it has the effect of severing a
defined portion of a jointly owned survey number and giving that
portion a separate identity in the revenue record, necessarily affects
the  proprietary  and  possessory  rights  of  every  recorded  co-
owner. Consent of all the co-owners is, therefore, a sine qua non for
such a consensual sub-division; in the absence of such consent,
separation of the joint property can only be achieved through lawful
partition  proceedings  before  the  competent  authority.  This
distinction assumes particular significance where the alienation is of a
specific portion of a joint survey number. A transferor may validly
alienate  his  undivided  proprietary  interest,  or  even  transfer
possession of a specific portion subject to adjustment, but he cannot,
by the mere act of alienation, unilaterally transform that portion into
a separately identified proprietary parcel to the exclusion of the
remaining co-sharers. Such transformation would, in substance and
effect, amount to partition without notice to, and without the consent
of,  the  other  recorded  owners.  The  principle  was  expressly
considered by this Court in Syed Muqaddar Shah and another v.
Haji Abdul Ahad Khan and another (1990 CLC 1596), where it was
observed that where the vendor was in hissadari possession of an
entire  khasra  number,  preparation  of  a  tatimma  allocating  a
particular portion thereof to the vendee would have the effect of
partitioning the khasra number without notice to and consent of the
other recorded co-owners. The Court further recognized that a sale
reflected in the column of cultivation, or preparation of a tatimma in
consequence of sale of a portion of hissadari possession, remains
subject to partition and adjustment of the rights of the recorded co-
sharers. The principle is also consistent with the law declared in Kala
Khan v. Shah Hussain and 9 others (1983 CLC 684), where this
Court held that sale of specific field numbers by a co-sharer remains
subject to adjustment in partition proceedings. The duty of the
authority conducting partition is to accommodate the vendee and
make good any deficiency, where possible, from the vendor’s other
joint land in the same estate. The rationale is simple: the co-sharer
cannot convey more than his proprietary entitlement, but neither can
the vendee be deprived of the benefit of a lawful purchase merely
because the vendor happened to be in possession of a particular
parcel at the time of sale. The rights of all concerned are ultimately
reconciled through lawful partition and adjustment. The principle
emerging from the above authorities may, therefore, be stated with
precision: a co-sharer is competent to alienate his proprietary interest
in the joint estate, and such alienation is valid to the extent of his total
entitlement therein; however, where a specific portion of a joint
survey number is identified for transfer, the identification of that
portion cannot, by preparation of a tatimma, be converted into a
separate proprietary parcel without the consent of all co-owners or
without a lawful partition. The transferee acquires no better title than
that possessed by the transferor, and the transfer remains subject to
the transferor’s total share in the estate and to adjustment at the time
of partition.
11.  Applying the above principles to the case in hand,
there is no dispute that other co-owners were recorded in the joint
property. Equally, there is nothing on record demonstrating that
those co-owners had consented to the preparation of the tatimma in
question. The order of the Revenue Officer endorsed on the reverse
of Mutation No. 857 dated 10.03.1979 does not disclose the consent
of all the recorded co-owners. In such circumstances, the Revenue
Officer could not, merely on the basis of the alienation made by
defendant No.1, confer upon the vendee a separate and identifiable
portion of the joint khasra by carving out a tatimma, because doing
so would have the legal effect of severing that portion from the joint
estate without completion of the process of partition. The learned
appellate  Court,  therefore,  committed  no  error  in  directing
cancellation of the tatimma. The cancellation of the tatimma does
not, in the circumstances of the case, amount to cancellation of the
underlying alienation. The two matters are legally distinct. The
alienation has been maintained, and rightly so, subject always to the
settled limitation that defendant No.1 could not transfer an interest
greater than his own proprietary entitlement in the entire estate. The
vendee, consequently, stepped into the shoes of defendant No.1 and
acquired the rights of the transferor only to the extent of his lawful
share. The specific portion mentioned in the transaction could
remain relevant for the purposes of possession and subsequent
adjustment, but it could not be treated as having been lawfully
severed from the joint property through an unauthorized tatimma.
12.  The  reliance  placed  by  learned  counsel  for  the
petitioners upon the proposition that a co-sharer is competent to
alienate his property is, therefore, unexceptionable as a general
proposition, but it does not advance the petitioners’ case on the
specific  question  before  this  Court.  The  issue  is  not  whether
defendant No.1 could alienate his proprietary interest. That question
has already been answered in favour of the petitioners, and the
respondents have not challenged that finding. The real question is
whether, as a consequence of that alienation, the Revenue Officer
could lawfully carve out a tatimma from a jointly owned survey
number without the consent of the other co-owners or a lawful
partition. The answer, in view of the settled legal position, must
necessarily be in the negative.
13.  Learned counsel for the petitioners also contended
that the joint property had subsequently been partitioned. In this
regard, the record produced through C.M. No. 1265 reflects the
order dated 27.09.2019 passed by Member-II, Board of Revenue,
whereby the appeal preferred by Ali Hassan Durrani against Mst.
Fahmida Begum and others was dismissed and the order of the
Additional Commissioner, Peshawar, was maintained. The order of
the Additional Commissioner, Peshawar, dated 10.12.2018 relates to
completion of the partition proceedings. The record further indicates
that Writ Petition No. 6546-P of 2019, whereby the order of the
Board of Revenue was questioned, was dismissed on 08.12.2018, and
the proceedings reflect completion of partition with effect from
10.08.2011.  These  subsequent  developments,  however,  do  not
retrospectively validate an otherwise unauthorized tatimma prepared
in  1979.  The  validity  of  the  impugned  revenue  act has  to  be
examined in the light of the legal position and the rights of the parties
existing  when  that  act  was  undertaken.  If  the  joint  estate  has
subsequently been partitioned, the rights flowing from the present
judgment  shall  necessarily  be  incorporated  and  adjusted  in  the
revenue record in accordance with the final determination of the
competent  forum.  The  subsequent  partition  may  regulate  the
ultimate  rights  and  possession  of  the  parties,  but  it  cannot
retrospectively convert an unauthorized unilateral sub-division into a
valid  consensual  partition.  It  is,  therefore,  clarified  that  the
cancellation of the tatimma does not preclude the vendee from
asserting such proprietary or possessory rights as may lawfully accrue
to her through the alienation made by defendant No.1. Those rights
shall, however, remain confined to the transferor’s total entitlement
in the joint estate and shall be worked out in accordance with the
final partition and adjustment of shares.
14.  There is another important aspect which cannot be
overlooked. The respondents instituted the suit in 1994, whereas the
impugned judgment of the learned appellate Court was rendered in
2012. The controversy relating to the tatimma, therefore, remained
sub judice before the civil Court during the intervening period. To
the extent the subsequent revenue proceedings purport to deal with
the same subject matter, the revenue entries must necessarily remain
subject to the adjudication of the competent civil Court. Once a civil
Court  has  adjudicated  upon  the  legality  of  the  act  affecting
proprietary rights, the revenue record cannot be permitted to operate
contrary to such adjudication.
15.  The cumulative effect of the foregoing discussion is
that three propositions govern the controversy: First, a co-sharer is
competent to alienate his undivided proprietary interest in the joint
estate, but he cannot transfer a title greater than his own; the validity
and  extent  of  the  transfer  are  necessarily  subject  to  his  total
entitlement in the entire estate. Second, where the transfer relates to
a specific portion of a jointly owned survey/khasra number, the
identification of that portion does not, by itself, effect partition or
sever the parcel from the joint estate. The transferee merely steps
into the shoes of the transferor and remains subject to the rights of
the  other  co-sharers  and  the  eventual  adjustment  in  partition.
Third, a tatimma, being a revenue mechanism for sub-dividing a
survey number and giving a distinct identity to a specified portion,
cannot lawfully be used as a substitute for partition. Where its
preparation has the effect of separating a specific portion from jointly
owned property, the consent of all co-owners is indispensable; failing
such  consent,  separation  can  only  be  achieved  through  lawful
partition proceedings conducted by the competent authority. The
learned appellate Court, therefore, correctly distinguished between
the underlying alienation and the consequential preparation of the
tatimma. While it preserved the alienation in favour of defendant
No.5, it rightly declined to recognize the unilateral severance of a
specific portion of the joint property through Mutation No. 857 and
the tatimma prepared pursuant thereto.
16.  For what has been discussed above, I find no illegality,
material  irregularity  or  jurisdictional  defect  in  the  impugned
judgment dated 11.10.2012 warranting interference in exercise of
revisional  jurisdiction  under  Section  115,  C.P.C.  The  learned
appellate Court has correctly applied the law to the facts of the case
and has drawn a legally sustainable distinction between the right of a
co-sharer  to  alienate  his  interest  and  the  impermissibility  of
unilaterally severing a specific portion of joint property through
preparation of a tatimma. Consequently, the instant Civil Revision
Petition,  being  devoid  of  merit,  is dismissed.  The  impugned
judgment  &  decree  dated  11.10.2012  passed  by  the  learned
Additional District Judge-VI, Peshawar, is maintained. It is, however,
clarified that cancellation of the tatimma shall not be construed as
cancellation of the underlying alienation in favour of defendant No.5,
which  shall  remain  subject  to  the  transferor’s  total  proprietary
entitlement in the joint estate and to the lawful adjustment of the
parties’ rights in partition proceedings. There shall be no order as to
costs.

JUDGMENT
MUHAMMAD NAEEM ANWAR, J.- Through the instant Civil
Revision  Petition,  the  petitioners  have  called  in  question  the
judgment  &  decree  dated 19.01.2015 passed  by  the  learned
Additional  District  Judge-IV,  Mardan,  whereby  the  appeal
preferred  against  the  judgment  and  decree
dated 06.12.2012 passed by the learned Civil Judge-XII, Mardan,
in consolidated Civil Suits No. 168/1 and 167/1, was dismissed.
2.  Before adverting to the controversy involved, it would be
appropriate to recapitulate the relevant facts and the chequered
history of the litigation in their chronological sequence, as the
matter has passed through various stages of adjudication and,
during the pendency of the present revision, certain persons were
brought on record pursuant to applications filed under section
12(2) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (“CPC”).
Background of the litigation.
3.  The  dispute  essentially  relates  to  the  ownership  and
revenue record of property originally claimed to have belonged
to Faiz Talab Khan, who had seven sons, namely, Bawar Khan,
Abdul Qadar Khan, Sher Afzal Khan, Mir Afzal Khan, Saadat
Khan, Mir Akbar Khan and Raidullah Khan. The plaintiffs of Civil
Suit  No. 168/1 instituted  their  suit  on 02.06.2009,  claiming
Zulfiqar Ali & others Vs Muhammad Amin Jan and others.
declaration  of  their  ownership  and  possession  on  the  basis
of registered deed No. 1828 dated 26.10.1946. Their case was that
the property had been transferred in their favour through the said
registered deed, but the corresponding entries in the revenue
record had either not been correctly incorporated or had been
recorded on the basis of registered deed No. 1829 instead of
registered deed No. 1828. They, therefore, sought declaration,
perpetual injunction against alienation of the property on the basis
of the allegedly erroneous revenue entries and correction of the
revenue record in accordance with their registered title. On the
other hand, the plaintiffs of Civil Suit No. 167/1 instituted a suit for
declaration and perpetual injunction, claiming themselves to be
owners in possession of 3000 kanals out of 31,023 kanals and 11
marlas of the disputed property. Both suits were contested by the
respective  defendants,  consolidated  and  tried  together.  After
recording of evidence and hearing the parties, the learned trial
Court, vide judgment and decree dated 06.12.2012, dismissed Civil
Suit No. 167/1, whereas Civil Suit No. 168/1 was decreed. The
judgment and decree of the learned trial Court were assailed
before the learned Additional District Judge-IV, Mardan, who, vide
judgment and decree dated 19.01.2015, dismissed the appeal and
maintained the findings of the learned trial Court. The aggrieved
parties consequently invoked the revisional jurisdiction of this
Court through Civil Revision No. 283-P/2015.
Compromise before this Court and subsequent proceedings.
4.  During  pendency  of the  Civil  Revision,  on 11.04.2016,
learned counsel appearing for the respective parties, namely, Mr.

167/1 may be decreed, both the registered deeds should
be incorporated in the revenue record to the extent of the
land mentioned therein. Parties may be left to bear their
own cost .”
On the basis of the aforesaid statement, the judgments and decrees

the  basis  of  the  compromise  and  the  admitted  existence  of

5.  Subsequently, however, added respondents, who were not

Service upon the original petitioners.
6.  There were six original petitioners, namely Zulfiqar Ali,

who were directed to be served through all possible modes. The
matter being considerably old, it was taken up in pursuance of the
order dated 22.06.2026. Mr. Aimal Khan Barkandi, Advocate,
informed the Court that although his late father, Mr. Maazullah
Khan Barkandi, Advocate, had represented the petitioners, after
his father's death he had no contact or instructions from the
petitioners. Learned counsel appearing for added respondents,
namely, M/S Muhammad Amir Rafiq, Shakeel Zada and Shahid
Zaman, Advocates, informed the Court that, as a compromise had
earlier been affected between the parties, respondents No. 23 and
30 were relevant to the controversy and were believed to be in
contact with the petitioners. Their cell numbers were accordingly
furnished to the Court, whereafter directions were issued for
service upon all the petitioners through all possible means. The
Moharrir Branch of the Civil Revision submitted a detailed report
regarding the efforts made for service through respondents No. 23
and 30. Cell numbers of petitioners No.2 and 3 were obtained and
were found switched on; however, when attempts were made to
contact them, the calls were not answered and the numbers were
subsequently switched off. Thereafter, the SHO, Police Station
Chura, Mardan, was also directed to effect service. The report
submitted by the police reflects service upon Afsar Ali s/o Quresh
Khan, (petitioner) bearing CNIC No. 16101-1148739-9 and cell
No. 03059693513. Despite such service, neither Afsar Ali nor his
brothers, sisters or mother, who are the remaining petitioners,
appeared before this Court. One Badal Khan, step-brother of
petitioners No.1 to 5 and step-son of Mst. Majmara, is present
before the Court. He is also the husband of respondent No. 33,
Mst.  Musarrat.  In  these  circumstances,  and  after  giving  due
consideration to the repeated efforts made to secure the presence
of the original petitioners and to protect the rights of all persons
who had subsequently come on record, the matter has been heard
on merits with the assistance of learned counsel who are present
before the Court.
Nature of the original transactions.
7.  The  controversy,  when  examined  against  its  historical
background,  revolves  around  two  registered  deeds,
namely, registered deed No. 1828 dated 26.10.1946 and registered
deed No. 1829 dated 26.10.1946, both stated to have emanated
from Faiz Talab Khan. The beneficiaries of registered deed No.
1828 were Abdul Qadar Khan, Sher Afzal Khan, Saadat Khan,
Mir Akbar Khan and Raidullah Khan, sons of Faiz Talab Khan,
whereas the beneficiaries of registered deed No. 1829 were the
grandsons of Faiz Talab Khan. Thus, both sets of claimants trace
their respective titles to the same predecessor, namely, Faiz Talab
Khan. It is significant that the plaintiffs of Civil Suit No. 167/1,
whose suit was dismissed by both Courts below, had not pleaded in
their plaint the material fact of acquisition of the property through
registered deed No. 1829. This omission substantially contributed
to the dismissal of their suit. Nevertheless, the relationship of the
beneficiaries of registered deed No. 1829 with Faiz Talab Khan has
not been disputed. Similarly, the status of the beneficiaries of
registered deed No. 1828 as sons of Faiz Talab Khan has not been
seriously controverted.
8.  The  plaintiffs  of  Civil  Suit  No.  168/1  principally
complained  that  although  their  registered  deed  had  been
incorporated in the revenue record, the shares and the source of
title had not been correctly reflected. According to them, the
revenue record erroneously reflected alienation in their favour on
the basis of registered deed No. 1829 rather than registered deed
No. 1828, necessitating judicial intervention for correction and
proper incorporation of their title. It is also apparent from the
record that the plaintiffs of Civil Suit No. 167/1 claimed rights to
the extent of 1800 kanals, whereas the plaintiffs of Civil Suit No.
168/1  claimed  approximately 8937  kanals from  the  disputed
property.  When  the  matter  came  before  this  Court  in  Civil
Revision No. 283-P/2015, the aforesaid position culminated in the
joint statement dated 11.04.2016, whereby the parties then before
the Court agreed that both registered deeds No. 1828 and 1829 be
given effect to in the revenue record to the extent of the land
mentioned therein. The difficulty, however, is that the matter did
not end there.
Emergence of the rights claimed by the added respondents.
9.  After the applicants under section 12(2) CPC were brought
on record, a materially different aspect of the controversy emerged.
The  added  respondents  have  asserted  rights  founded
upon mutation No. 1107, which, according to learned counsel
representing them, was attested pursuant to a decision of the
Deputy Commissioner in Suit No. 8/6 dated 01.06.1949 and was
subsequently incorporated in the revenue record. This assertion is
of considerable legal and factual significance. It is not merely a
collateral objection to the compromise between the original parties.
Rather, it constitutes an independent claim concerning the source,
extent and subsistence of title in the disputed property. Learned
counsel representing the added respondents were specifically asked
whether  the  alienation  made  by  Faiz  Talab  Khan  through
registered  deeds  No.  1828  and  1829  could  subsequently  be
questioned by the legal heirs of Faiz Talab Khan. In response, Mr.
Shakeel Zada, learned Advocate, produced a copy of mutation No.
1107. Conversely, learned counsel representing respondents No.
10 to 18 contended that Faiz Talab Khan had ceased to be the
owner of the property before execution and registration of the
deeds in 1946. The first aspect, namely, whether an owner could
alienate his property in favour of one or more persons, including to
the exclusion of his legal heirs, is no longer res integra. The
Hon'ble  Supreme  Court  in Ghulam  Muhammad  v.  Mian
Muhammad and another (2007 SCMR 231) has recognized the
unfettered right of an owner to alienate his property in favour of
any person, including by excluding one or more of his prospective
legal  heirs.  That  principle,  however,  does  not  conclude  the
controversy before us.
Why the matter requires remand.
10.  The decisive question is not simply whether registered
deeds No. 1828 and 1829 were executed and registered in 1946.
Nor is the controversy confined to the abstract proposition whether
Faiz Talab Khan could alienate his property in favour of some of
his descendants. The real difficulty is that mutation No. 1107 and
the rights claimed thereunder were neither part of the original
pleadings nor the subject of adjudication before the Courts below.
The persons now asserting rights on the strength of that mutation
were not parties to the original litigation in a manner enabling their
independent claims to be properly pleaded, controverted and
adjudicated upon. Their assertions, therefore, could not have been
effectively examined either by the learned trial Court or by the
learned appellate Court. Equally important is the fact that, while
exercising revisional jurisdiction, this Court cannot satisfactorily
determine disputed questions of title which require examination of
the original revenue record, the basis and legality of mutation No.
1107, the nature and extent of the alleged alienation thereunder,
the relationship between the parties, the effect of the registered
deeds and the competing claims of the persons deriving title from
the beneficiaries thereof, without affording all concerned parties a
meaningful opportunity to plead their respective cases and lead
evidence. The issue is particularly important because the added
respondents do not merely seek to support or oppose the original
judgments. They assert independent and substantive rights in the
property. Those rights may have a direct bearing upon the validity,
extent and legal effect of the title claimed by the plaintiffs of both
suits. Therefore, any attempt by this Court to finally determine
those questions on the basis of the material presently available
would not only amount to adjudicating disputed questions of fact in
revisional jurisdiction but would also deprive the concerned parties
of the opportunity to establish their respective claims through
pleadings, production of the original revenue record and evidence.
11.  The Court is conscious that litigation must ordinarily attain
finality and that remand should not be ordered merely to provide a
party with an opportunity to fill gaps in its case. A remand becomes
necessary, however, where an issue essential to the determination
of the real controversy has emerged at a subsequent stage, where
persons asserting independent rights have been brought on record,
and where such rights were neither pleaded nor adjudicated upon
by the Courts below. The present case falls squarely within that
category.
The stance of the added respondents, particularly those claiming
through mutation No. 1107 and those claiming as vendees from
Saadat Khan and Sher Afzal Khan, was not before the Courts
below. Consequently, there was no occasion for those Courts to
examine the validity and legal effect of mutation No. 1107; the
source and extent of the title claimed thereunder; its relationship
with  registered  deeds  No.  1828  and  1829;  the  alleged  prior
alienation  by  Faiz  Talab  Khan;  or  the  effect  of  subsequent
alienations made by the beneficiaries of the registered deeds.
These are not matters which can appropriately be determined for
the first time in revisional jurisdiction. They require a proper trial,
based upon complete pleadings, production of the relevant original
revenue record and registration record, and an opportunity to all
concerned parties to lead evidence and rebut the case of their
opponents.
Necessary amendment and impleadment.
12.  In order to bring the controversy to its logical conclusion
and to avoid further rounds of litigation, it is necessary that the
pleadings themselves reflect the complete dispute now emerging
from the record. Accordingly, the plaintiffs of both Civil Suit No.
167/1 and Civil Suit No. 168/1 shall be permitted to amend their
respective plaints, to the extent necessary for incorporating the
subsequent developments and the claims of the persons now
brought on record. In particular, the plaintiffs shall array all the
added respondents/applicants who have been brought on record in
consequence of the proceedings under section 12(2) CPC as
defendants in their respective suits. The plaintiffs of Civil Suit No.
167/1 shall also be at liberty to appropriately amend their prayer so
as to clearly and comprehensively seek the relief which they claim
on the basis of registered deed No. 1829 and the subsequent
proceedings, rather than leaving the real nature of their claim to be
inferred from the material brought before this Court. Both sets of
plaintiffs shall submit their amended plaints before the learned trial
Court,  whereafter  all  the  defendants,  including  the  added
respondents,  shall  be  afforded  an  opportunity  to  file  their
respective written statements and to raise all lawful pleas available
to them.
Scope of adjudication on remand.
13.  The  learned  trial  Court  shall  thereafter  adjudicate  the
consolidated suits afresh, strictly in accordance with law and on the
basis of the pleadings and evidence brought on record. Without
limiting the jurisdiction of the learned trial Court, the following
material questions shall receive specific consideration:
i.  Whether  registered  deeds  No.  1828  and  1829  dated
26.10.1946 were validly executed, registered and attested and what
property and shares were transferred thereunder;
ii.  Whether  Faiz  Talab  Khan  was  the  lawful  owner  of  the
property, or of the relevant shares therein, at the time of execution and
registration of the aforesaid deeds;
iii.  The nature, origin, validity and legal effect of mutation No.
1107, including the decision of the Deputy Commissioner in Suit No.
8/6 dated 01.06.1949 on the basis of which the mutation is stated to
have been attested;
iv.  Whether mutation No. 1107 confers, recognizes or records
any right, title or interest in favour of the added respondents and, if so,
the extent and legal character of such rights;
v.  Whether the rights claimed on the basis of mutation No. 1107
are inconsistent with, subsequent to, or otherwise affected by registered
deeds No. 1828 and 1829;
vi.  Whether the beneficiaries of registered deeds No. 1828 and
1829 acquired valid title to the extent claimed by them and whether
the subsequent alienations made by Saadat Khan and Sher Afzal Khan
in favour of the persons now arrayed as defendants created any legally
enforceable rights;
vii.  Whether the fact that neither Bawar Khan nor his legal heirs,
during his lifetime or thereafter within the relevant period, challenged
the registered deeds No. 1828 and 1829 has any legal consequence for
the claims now advanced by his legal heirs, and if so, to what extent;
viii.  Whether the revenue entries correctly reflect the registered
instruments  and  the  actual  title  of  the  parties,  and  whether  any
correction,  cancellation,  incorporation  or  consequential  relief  is
warranted; and
ix.  Ultimately,  what  relief,  if  any,  each  of  the  plaintiffs  and
defendants is entitled to in accordance with law.
The learned trial Court shall not treat any observation made
herein  as  a  final  determination  of  the  aforesaid  factual
controversies. All parties shall remain at liberty to establish their
respective claims by producing admissible evidence, including the
original registered deeds, the complete revenue record, mutation
No. 1107, the proceedings and order on the basis of which the
mutation was attested, and any other relevant documentary or oral
evidence permissible in law.
Conclusion.
14.  For the foregoing reasons, this Court is of the considered
view that the judgments and decrees of the learned Courts below
cannot be allowed to stand in their present form. The principal
reason for remand is not merely the existence of a disputed claim
but the emergence, after the earlier adjudication, of independent
claims of persons who were not afforded an opportunity before the
Courts below to plead and establish their rights, particularly rights
asserted on the strength of mutation No. 1107 and subsequent
transactions. Those claims may materially affect the determination
of title and the relief sought by the plaintiffs. Their adjudication
without a proper trial would offend the fundamental requirement
of  fair  opportunity  and  would  also  risk  an  incomplete  or
inconclusive determination of the real controversy. The purpose of
remand,  therefore,  is  to  ensure  that  the  entire  dispute  is
decided once and for all, on the basis of complete pleadings and
evidence and after hearing every person whose proprietary rights
may be affected by the ultimate judgment, rather than permitting
the litigation to generate another round of proceedings at a later
stage.
15.  Consequently, the instant Civil Revision Petition is allowed.
The judgment and decree dated 19.01.2015 passed by the learned
Additional  District  Judge-IV,  Mardan,  and  the  judgment  and
decree dated 06.12.2012 passed by the learned Civil Judge-XII,
Mardan, in Civil Suits No. 168/1 and 167/1, are set aside. Both
suits are remanded to the learned trial Court for fresh adjudication
in  accordance  with  the  observations  and  directions  contained
herein. The plaintiffs of Civil Suit No. 167/1 and Civil Suit No.
168/1 shall file their respective amended plaints before the learned
trial Court, incorporating such amendments as are necessary to
disclose their complete claims and, in particular, the plaintiffs of
Civil Suit No. 167/1 shall be at liberty to amend their prayer
appropriately. All added respondents/applicants who have been
brought on record in the proceedings under section 12(2) CPC
shall be arrayed as defendants in the respective suits. Thereafter,
the defendants shall be granted reasonable opportunity to submit
their written statements and the learned trial Court shall frame
appropriate issues on the basis of the amended pleadings. The
learned trial Court shall decide the consolidated suits afresh after
affording all parties a full and effective opportunity to produce
documentary and oral evidence and to rebut the case of the
opposite side. The Court shall examine the competing claims
arising from registered deeds No. 1828 and 1829, mutation No.
1107 and subsequent transactions independently and strictly in
accordance with law. Nothing contained in this judgment shall be
construed as an expression of opinion on the ultimate merits of
any of those claims. The learned trial Court shall bear in mind that
the litigation is considerably old. It shall, therefore, proceed with
expedition and shall not grant unnecessary adjournments. Subject
to the cooperation of the parties and ordinary exigencies of judicial
proceedings, the learned trial Court shall endeavour to conclude
the proceedings within five months from the date of submission of
the amended plaints. The parties shall appear before the learned
Civil Judge-XII, Mardan, on 12.09.2026. Parties are left to bear
their own costs.

8.  As regards Question No.2 , its determination
requires  a  brief  examination  of  the  historical
development and legislative purpose of the outsider
quota  in  trade  union  law.  The  earliest  relevant
enactment was the  Indian Trade Unions Act, 1926 .
Section 22 thereof originally provided:
“22. Proportion of officers to be connected with the
industry.  Not less than one-half of the total number of
the officers of every registered Trade Union shall be
persons actually engaged or employed in an industry
with which the Trade Union is connected: Provided
that the Local Government may, by special or general
order, declare that the provisions of this section shall
not apply to any Trade Union or class of Trade Unions
specified in the order.”
A plain reading of the original provision shows that at
least one-half of the officers were required to be
actually engaged or employed in the industry with
which the union was connected. The provision did not
expressly prescribe any qualification or condition for
the remaining half, who could be drawn from outside
the concerned industry. Section 22 was subsequently
substituted  by  section  3  of  the  Trade  Unions
(Amendment) Ordinance, 1961 , as follows:
“22. Election of officers. (1)  No person shall be
elected as an officer of the Trade Union who—
(a)  has at any time been convicted of an offence under
this Act or under section 23 or section 24 of the
Industrial Disputes Ordinance, 1959; or
(b)  has at any time been dismissed from employment
in any industry on the ground of conviction for an
offence—
(i)  involving,  in  the  opinion  of  the  appropriate
Government, moral turpitude; or
(ii)  relating to activities prejudicial to the defence or
security of Pakistan; or
(c)  in the case of a person referred to in sub-section (2),
is not a paid full-time Trade Union worker.
(2)  A registered Trade Union shall not elect more than
twenty-five per cent of the total number of its officers
from amongst the persons who are not, for the time
being, employed or engaged in the industry with which
the Trade Union is connected:
Provided that the Central Government may, on the
grounds of security, by notification in the official
Gazette, prohibit any Trade Union in any industry
connected with defence carried on by or under the
authority of the Central Government, from electing any
officer from amongst such persons.”
This  amendment  reduced  the  permissible
representation of outsiders from one-half to twenty-five
per cent and, through section 22(1)(c), clarified that the
remaining seventy-five per cent of the executive body
were to be paid full-time trade union workers. No
separate  qualification  or  condition,  however,  was
prescribed for the twenty-five per cent outsider quota.
The said law remained in force until 1968, when it was
repealed  by  the  West  Pakistan  Trade  Unions
Ordinance, 1968 . The subject previously dealt with
under section 22 was substantially re-enacted in section
29 of the new Ordinance, which was almost  pari
materia  with the earlier provision and read as follows:
“29. (1)  No person shall be elected as an officer of a
registered Trade Union, who—
(a)  has at any time within the preceding 3 years, been
convicted of an offence under this Ordinance or under
section 23 or section 24 of the Industrial Disputes
Ordinance, 1959 (Ordinance LVI of 1959), or under
section 30 or section 31 of the West Pakistan Industrial
Disputes Ordinance, 1968;
(b)  has been convicted of an offence involving moral
turpitude, or for any act prejudicial to the defence or
security of Pakistan, or public order, and sentenced to
imprisonment for a term of not less than 6 months,
unless 3 years or such less period as Government may,
by notification in the official Gazette, specify in this
behalf has elapsed from the date of expiration of the
sentence.
(2)  A registered Trade Union, shall elect not less than
75 per centum of the total number of its officers, from
amongst the persons, who are for the time being
employed or engaged in the industry with which the
Trade Union is connected.
(3)  No person shall be elected as an officer of a
registered Trade Union who has not—
(a)  in case he is employed or engaged in the industry
with which the Trade Union is concerned attained the
age of 21 years; and
(b)  in any other case, attained the age of 25 years, and
is a paid full time Trade Union worker.”
Section 29 lays down three basic rules regarding the
election of trade union officers. First, a person cannot
be elected if, during the preceding three years, he has
been convicted of specified labour offences. A person
convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, or of
an act prejudicial to the defence, security or public
order of Pakistan, is also disqualified for the prescribed
period. Second, at least seventy-five per cent of the
officers  must  be  persons  presently  employed  or
engaged in the industry with which the trade union is
connected. This means that not more than twenty-five
per cent may come from outside that industry. Third, a
person employed in the relevant industry must be at
least twenty-one years of age. A person coming from
outside the industry must be at least twenty-five years
of age and must be a paid full-time trade union worker.
Thus, while the twenty-five per cent quota was retained,
the 1968 Ordinance also imposed express qualifications
upon an outsider: he had to be at least twenty-five years
of  age  and  a  paid  full-time  trade  union  worker.
However, the  West Pakistan Trade Unions Ordinance,
1968  was shortly thereafter repealed by section 67 of
the  Industrial Relations Ordinance, 1969  (IRO 1969) .
The subject was then dealt with in section 7(d), which
provided:
“ 7. Requirements for registration . A trade union
shall  not  be  entitled  to  registration  under  this
Ordinance unless the constitution thereof provides for
the following matters, namely:—
(a) the name and address of the trade union;
(b) the objects for which the trade union has been
formed;
(c) the purposes for which the general funds of the
union shall be applicable;
(d) the names of the persons forming the executive
committee including the officers, of whom seventy-
five per cent shall be from amongst the workers
actually engaged or employed in the establishment
with which the trade union is connected, and the
manner of their election;
(e) the conditions under which a member shall be
entitled to any benefit assured by the constitution of the
trade union and under which any fine or forfeiture may
be imposed on him;
(f) the maintenance of a list of the members of the trade
union and of adequate facilities for the inspection
thereof by the officers and members of the trade union;
(g) the manner in which the constitution shall be
amended, varied or rescinded;
(h) the safe custody of the funds of the trade union, its
annual  audit,  the  manner  of  audit  and  adequate
facilities for inspection of the account books by the
officers and members of the trade union; and
(i) the manner in which the trade union may be
dissolved.”
A bare reading of section 7 shows that at least seventy-
five per cent of the executive had to consist of workers
actually  employed  in  the  establishment  concerned,
leaving the remaining twenty-five per cent for persons
from outside it. Unlike the 1968 Ordinance, section 7
neither required such outsiders to be paid full-time trade
union  workers  nor  prescribed  any  separate  age
qualification for them. The IRO 1969 remained in force
until 2002 when it was repealed by section 80 of the
Industrial Relations Ordinance, 2002  (IRO 2002) . The
subject previously dealt with under section 7 was
substantially  re-enacted  in  Section  6  of  the  new
Ordinance and read as follows:
“ 6. Requirements for registration . (1) A trade union
shall  not  be  entitled  to  registration  under  this
Ordinance unless the constitution thereof provides for
the following matters, namely:—
(a) the name and address of the trade union;
(b) the objects for which the trade union has been
formed;
(c) the purposes for which the general funds of the
union shall be applicable;
(d) the number of persons forming the executive which
shall not exceed the prescribed limit and shall include
not less than seventy-five per cent from amongst the
workmen  actually  engaged  or  employed  in  the
establishment or establishments or the industry for
which the trade union has been formed;
(e) the conditions under which a member shall be
entitled to any benefit assured by the constitution of the
trade union and under which any fine or forfeiture may
be imposed on him;
(f) the maintenance of a list of the members of the trade
union and of adequate facilities for the inspection
thereof by the office-bearers and members of the trade
union;
(g) the manner in which the constitution shall be
amended, varied or rescinded;
(h) the safe custody of the funds of the trade union, its
annual  audit,  the  manner  of  audit  and  adequate
facilities for inspection of the account books by the
office-bearers and members of the trade union;
(i) the manner in which the trade union may be
dissolved;
(j) the manner of election of office-bearers by the
general body of the trade union and the term, not
exceeding three years, for which an office-bearer may
hold office upon his election or re-election through
secret ballot;
(k) the procedure for expressing want of confidence in
any office-bearer of the trade union; and
(l) the meetings of the executive and of the general
body of the trade union, so that the executive shall meet
at least once in every four months and the general body
at least once every year.
(2) Without prejudice to the provisions of sub-section
(1), a trade union of workmen shall not be entitled to
registration under this Ordinance—
(a)  unless  all  its  members  are  workmen  actually
engaged or employed in the establishment or industry
with which the trade union is connected; and
(b) where there are two or more registered trade unions
in  the  establishment,  group  of  establishments  or
industry with which the trade union is connected,
unless it has as its members not less than one-fourth of
the  total  number  of  workmen  employed  in  such
establishment, group of establishments or industry, as
the case may be.”
A bare reading of Section 6 shows that it prescribes the
essential conditions for the registration of a trade union.
Sub-section (1) requires its constitution to contain the
specified matters, including not less than seventy-five
per cent representation of workmen in the executive,
whereas  sub-section  (2)  imposes  the  additional
conditions that all members must be workmen of the
concerned establishment or industry and, where two or
more unions already exist, the applicant union must
represent at least one-fourth of the total workforce.
Thus,  clause  (d)  of  sub-section  (1)  concerns  the
composition of the executive, while clause (a) of sub-
section (2) governs the general membership of the
union. The IRO 2002, therefore, retained the same ratio
without prescribing any independent qualification for
the remaining twenty-five per cent of the executive
under clause (d). This law remained in force until its
repeal by section 87 of the  Industrial Relations Act,
2008  (IRA  2008) .  The  IRA  2008,  however,
substantially retained the scheme embodied in section
6 of the IRO 2002.

It is a settled principle of statutory interpretation that
where  the  language  of  a  provision  is  clear  and
unambiguous, it must be given its plain meaning.
Accordingly, in the absence of any express statutory
qualification for outsiders, none can be read into the
law, for the Court cannot supply an omission or add
words  which  the  legislature  has  deliberately  not
enacted.  T he  superior  courts  of  Pakistan  have
consistently upheld the "plain meaning" rule and the
doctrine of  casus omissus ,
establishing that when the
language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, it must
be applied as written. Courts are strictly prohibited
from adding words, supplying omissions, or reading in
qualifications that the legislature did not explicitly
enact, as doing so would constitute legislation rather
than  judicial  construction.  In  Syed  Saad  Ali  v.
Federation of Pakistan  (2026 SCMR 22) , the Supreme
Casus omissus  is a Latin expression meaning  “an omitted case”  or  “a
situation not provided for by the law.”  In statutory interpretation, it refers
to a matter that the legislature has unintentionally or deliberately left out of
a statute. Courts generally cannot fill such a gap by adding words to the
law; the omission must ordinarily be corrected by the legislature itself.
Court explained the principle of statutory interpretation
in the following terms:
“The function of the judiciary is not to legislate or
question the wisdom of the legislature in making a
particular law, nor can it refuse to enforce a law. The
doctrine of severability permits the court to sever the
unconstitutional portion of a partially unconstitutional
statute  in  order  to  preserve  the  operation  of  any
uncontested or valid remainder. The words contained
in  statutes,  rules,  or  regulations,  are  first  to  be
understood in their natural, ordinary, or popular sense,
and phrases and sentences are construed according to
their grammatical meaning, unless that leads to some
absurdity or unless there is something in the context,
or in the object of the statute, to suggest the contrary.”
In  Collector of Customs, Model Customs Collectorate,
Peshawar v. Waseef Ullah  (2023 SCMR 503) , the
Supreme Court explained the rule of plain construction
in the following terms:
“If the words of the section are plain and unambiguous,
then  there  is  no  question  of  interpretation  or
construction.  The  duty  of  the  Court  then  is  to
implement those provisions with no hesitation. When
the material words are capable of two constructions,
one of which is likely to defeat or impair the policy of
the Act whilst the other construction is likely to assist
the achievement of the said policy, then the Courts
would prefer to adopt the latter construction. The Court
cannot supply casus omissus and while interpreting a
statute, the Court cannot fill in gaps or rectify defects
and cannot add words to a statute or read words into it
which are not there, especially when the literal reading
produces  an  intelligible  result.  The  legal  maxim,
"absoluta  sententia  expositore  non  indigent"  also
reminds us that, when the language is not only plain,
but  admits  of  but  one  meaning,  the  task  of
interpretation can hardly be said to arise. It is not
allowable  to  interpret  what  has  no  need  of
interpretation.  Whereas  another  maxim  "generalia
verba  sunt  generalita  intelligenda"  expresses  that
general words are to be understood generally and what
is generally spoken shall be generally understood
unless it be qualified by some special subsequent
words or unless there is in the statute itself some
ground for restricting their meaning by reasonable
construction, not by arbitrary addition or retrenchment
[Ref: N. S. Bindra's interpretation of Statutes (Tenth
Edition), (Page No.609-610) and (Page No.656-657)].
In  Nadeem Ahmed, Advocate v. Federation of Pakistan
(2013 SCMR 1062) , the Supreme Court expounded the
principle of statutory interpretation in the following
terms:
“A Casus Omissus can, in no case, be supplied by the
Court of law as that would amount to altering the
provision. "It is not our function, as was held by Mr.
Justice Walsh, in the case of "Attorney-General v.
Bihari, re Australia Factors Limited (1966) 67 S.R. (N.
S. W) 150; to repair the blunders that are to be found
in the legislation". They must be corrected by the
legislator". A Court of law is not entitled to read words
into the Constitution or an Act of Parliament unless
clear reason is found within the four corners of either
of them.”
Thus, in the light of the principles enunciated above,
section 6 of the IRA 2010 does not prescribe any
qualification, restriction or condition in respect of the
remaining twenty-five per cent of the executive body
who may be drawn from outside the establishment or
industry concerned. The provision is clear and admits
of  no  ambiguity.  Accordingly,  no  additional
qualification or limitation can be introduced through
judicial interpretation, as doing so would amount to
supplying a  casus omissus  and rewriting the statute.
The  provision  must,  therefore,  be  given  effect  in
accordance with its plain and ordinary meaning, and the
legislative choice embodied therein must be respected
and enforced as enacted.

employees of a department of the Government or local
authority who belong to the superior, managerial,
secretarial, directorial, supervisory or agency staff and
who have been notified for this purpose in the official
Gazette shall be deemed to fall within the category of
“employers”;
Section 2(xxxiii) — “Worker” and “Workman”
“worker” and “workman” mean a person not falling
within the definition of employer who is employed
(including  employment  as  a  supervisor  or  as  an
apprentice) in an establishment or industry for hire or
reward either directly or through a contractor whether
the terms of employment be express or implied, and,
for the purpose of any proceedings under this Act in
relation to an industrial dispute includes a person who
has been dismissed, discharged, retrenched, laid-off or
otherwise removed from employment in connection
with or as a consequence of that dispute or whose
dismissal, discharge, retrenchment, lay-off, or removal
has led to that dispute but does not include any person
who  is  employed  mainly  in  managerial  or
administrative capacity.
Under  section  2(vii)  of  the  IRA  2010,  the  term
“employer” includes any person responsible for hiring
or recruitment, or for the management, supervision or
control of an establishment, as well as any person
concerned  with  the  management  of  its  affairs.  In
relation to a Government department or local authority,
however,  officers  belonging  to  the  superior,
managerial,  secretarial,  directorial,  supervisory  or
agency staff are deemed to be employers only if they
have been duly notified as such in the official Gazette.
Conversely,  section  2(xxxiii)  defines  a  worker  or
workman as a person employed for hire or reward who
does not fall within the definition of employer and is
not mainly engaged in managerial or administrative
functions.  The  definition  expressly  includes  a
supervisor or apprentice. Thus, supervisory status by
itself does not exclude a person from the category of
worker; exclusion arises only where the person mainly
performs managerial or administrative functions or
otherwise satisfies the statutory definition of employer.
The status of respondent No.3 cannot, therefore, be
determined merely from his Grade 17 designation. The
status of respondent No.3 must be determined from the
actual nature of his duties, the extent of his managerial,
administrative or supervisory authority, his role in
hiring or recruitment and, where applicable, whether he
has been duly notified as an employer in the official
Gazette. No material is available on the record to
establish that respondent No. 3 acquired the status of an
employer merely upon his placement in Grade 17.
12 .  Thus, for the foregoing reasons, Question
No.2 is answered in negative. Section 6 of the IRA 2010
does  not  prescribe  any  qualification  or  restriction
requiring a person inducted against the twenty-five per
cent  outsiders’  quota  to  fall  within  the  statutory
definition of a worker or workman. In the absence of
any such express requirement, no additional condition
can be introduced through interpretation. Respondent
No.3 was, therefore, not required to possess the status
of a worker or workman for induction against the said
quota.  Consequently,  the  instant  Labour  Revision,
being devoid of merit, is dismissed.
