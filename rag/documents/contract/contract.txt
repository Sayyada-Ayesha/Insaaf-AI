[SECTION: 1] The Contract Act, 1872 (Act IX of 1872) — Short title, extent and commencement. This Act may be called the Contract Act, 1872. It extends to the whole of Pakistan and applies to all contracts made on or after 1 September 1872. Source: The Pakistan Code (pakistancode.gov.pk). Status: CURRENT. Provisions: Sections 1–238.

[SECTION: 2] Contract Act — Interpretation Clause. (a) Proposal (offer): when one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything with a view to obtaining the assent of that other; (b) Promise: proposal when accepted becomes a promise; (c) Promisor/promisee: person making / accepting the proposal; (d) Consideration: when at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing something; (e) Agreement: every promise and every set of promises forming consideration for each other; (f) Contract: agreement enforceable by law; (g) Void agreement: not enforceable by law; (h) Voidable contract: enforceable at option of one party; (i) Contingent contract: to do or not to do upon happening of uncertain event.

[SECTION: 3] Communication, acceptance and revocation of proposals — Section 3: Communication of proposal, acceptance and revocation are deemed to be made by any act or omission of the party proposing, accepting or revoking by which he intends to communicate or which has the effect of communicating.

[SECTION: 4] Communication when complete — Communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made. Communication of acceptance is complete as against the proposer when put in course of transmission to him; as against the acceptor when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer. Revocation is complete against the person who makes it when it is put in course of transmission; against the person to whom it is made when it comes to his knowledge.

[SECTION: 5] Revocation of proposals and acceptances — A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer. An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards.

[SECTION: 6] How revocation is made — A proposal is revoked by: (1) notice of revocation by the proposer; (2) lapse of time prescribed or if no time prescribed, by lapse of reasonable time; (3) failure of condition precedent; (4) death or insanity of the proposer, if the fact of death or insanity comes to the knowledge of the acceptor before acceptance.

[SECTION: 7] Acceptance must be absolute — In order to convert a proposal into a promise, the acceptance must be absolute and unqualified, and must be expressed in some usual and reasonable manner unless the proposal prescribes the manner of acceptance.

[SECTION: 8] Acceptance by performing condition — Performance of the conditions of a proposal, or the acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise which may be offered with a proposal, is an acceptance.

[SECTION: 9] Express and implied promises — Promises may be express (made by words spoken or written) or implied (inferred from the conduct of the parties or from circumstances).

[SECTION: 10] What agreements are contracts — All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not expressly declared to be void.

[SECTION: 11] Who are competent to contract — Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority (18 years in Pakistan), who is of sound mind, and who is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject.

[SECTION: 12] Sound mind — A person is said to be of sound mind if at the time of making the contract he is capable of understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon his interests.

[SECTION: 13] Free consent — Two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense.

[SECTION: 14] Free consent defined — Consent is said to be free when it is not caused by: (1) coercion, (2) undue influence, (3) fraud, (4) misrepresentation, or (5) mistake.

[SECTION: 15] Coercion — Coercion is the committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the Pakistan Penal Code, or the unlawful detaining or threatening to detain any property, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement.

[SECTION: 16] Undue influence — A contract is said to be induced by undue influence where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of the parties is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over the other.

[SECTION: 17] Fraud — Fraud means and includes any of the following acts by a party to a contract or with his connivance: (a) suggestion of a fact which is not true; (b) active concealment of a fact; (c) promise made without intention of performing it; (d) any other act fitted to deceive; (e) any such act or omission as the law specially declares to be fraudulent.

[SECTION: 18] Misrepresentation — Misrepresentation means: (a) a positive assertion of a fact not true but believed to be true; (b) any breach of duty which gives an advantage to the person committing it by misleading the other; (c) causing a party to make a mistake as to the substance of the thing which is the subject of the agreement.

[SECTION: 19] Voidability of agreements without free consent — When consent to an agreement is caused by coercion, fraud or misrepresentation, the agreement is a contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused.

[SECTION: 20] Void agreements due to mistake — Where both parties to an agreement are under a mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is void.

[SECTION: 23] Lawful consideration and object — The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful unless it is: forbidden by law, or would defeat the provisions of any law, or is fraudulent, or involves injury to the person or property of another, or the court regards it as immoral or opposed to public policy.

[SECTION: 25] Agreement without consideration void — An agreement made without consideration is void, unless: (1) it is expressed in writing and registered under the Registration Act and is made on account of natural love and affection between parties standing in near relation; (2) it is a promise to compensate for something voluntarily done; (3) it is a promise to pay a debt barred by limitation law.

[SECTION: 26] Agreement in restraint of marriage void — Every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void.

[SECTION: 27] Agreement in restraint of trade void — Every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is void to that extent. Exceptions: sale of goodwill, partnership non-compete during term.

[SECTION: 28] Agreements in restraint of legal proceedings void — Agreements limiting time to enforce rights, restricting parties from enforcing rights through courts, or discharging parties from liability for breach, are void.

[SECTION: 29] Agreements void for uncertainty — Agreements, the meaning of which is not certain or capable of being made certain, are void.

[SECTION: 30] Wagering agreements void — Agreements by way of wager are void. No suit shall be brought for recovering anything alleged to be won on a wager.

[SECTION: 31] Contingent contracts — A contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something if some event collateral to the contract does or does not happen.

[SECTION: 32] Enforcement of contingent contracts — Contingent contracts to do or not to do anything if an uncertain future event happens cannot be enforced unless the event has happened. If the event becomes impossible, such contracts become void.

[SECTION: 37] Obligation of parties — Parties to a contract must either perform or offer to perform their respective promises, unless such performance is dispensed with or excused under the provisions of this Act or of any other law.

[SECTION: 38] Effect of refusal to accept offer — Where a promisor has made an offer of performance and the promisee does not accept it within a reasonable time, the promisor is not responsible for non-performance nor loses his rights under the contract.

[SECTION: 39] Effect of refusal of party to perform — When a party to a contract has refused to perform or disabled himself from performing his promise in its entirety, the other party may put an end to the contract.

[SECTION: 40] Personal performance — A contract involving personal skill or confidence must be performed by the promisor personally unless the contract allows delegation.

[SECTION: 53] Time for performance — Where no time is specified for performance, the promise must be performed within a reasonable time.

[SECTION: 54] Reciprocal promises — When a contract consists of reciprocal promises to be simultaneously performed, no promisor need perform his promise unless the promisee is ready and willing to perform his reciprocal promise.

[SECTION: 55] Effect of failure to perform at fixed time — When a party fails to perform at the stipulated time and the contract is not void due to time being essential, the contract is voidable at the option of the other party if the intention was that time should be essential.

[SECTION: 56] Doctrine of frustration — An agreement to do an impossible act is void. A contract to do an act which becomes impossible or unlawful after the contract is made becomes void when the impossibility or unlawfulness occurs.

[SECTION: 62] Effect of novation, rescission and alteration — If the parties to a contract agree to substitute a new contract or to rescind or alter it, the original contract need not be performed.

[SECTION: 63] Promisee may dispense with performance — Every promisee may dispense with or remit wholly or in part the performance of the promise made to him, or may extend the time for such performance, or may accept instead of it any satisfaction which he thinks fit.

[SECTION: 64] Consequences of rescission of voidable contract — When a person at whose option a contract is voidable rescinds it, the other party need not perform his promise. The party rescinding must restore any benefit received from the other party.

[SECTION: 65] Obligation of person who has received advantage under void agreement — When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract must restore it or make compensation for it.

[SECTION: 67] Indemnity — A contract of indemnity is a contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself or by the conduct of any other person.

[SECTION: 124] Indemnity defined — A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor or any other person is called a contract of indemnity.

[SECTION: 126] Guarantee defined — A contract of guarantee is a contract to perform the promise or discharge the liability of a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is called the surety, the person for whom the guarantee is given is called the principal debtor, and the person to whom the guarantee is given is called the creditor.

[SECTION: 127] Consideration for guarantee — Anything done or any promise made for the benefit of the principal debtor may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee.

[SECTION: 128] Surety's liability — The liability of the surety is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, unless it is otherwise provided by the contract.

[SECTION: 133] Discharge of surety — Any variance made without the surety's consent in the terms of the contract between the principal debtor and the creditor discharges the surety as to transactions subsequent to the variance.

[SECTION: 134] Discharge by release of principal debtor — The surety is discharged if the creditor releases the principal debtor, or does any act inconsistent with the rights of the surety, or omits to do any act which his duty to the surety requires.

[SECTION: 135] Discharge by creditor's act or omission — The surety is discharged if the creditor loses or parts with any security given to him by the principal debtor without the surety's consent.

[SECTION: 148] Bailment defined — A bailment is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall be returned or disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them when the purpose is accomplished.

[SECTION: 149] Delivery to bailee — The delivery to the bailee may be actual or constructive.

[SECTION: 150] Bailor's duty — The bailor is bound to disclose to the bailee faults in the goods bailed of which the bailor is aware and which materially interfere with their use or expose the bailee to extraordinary risks.

[SECTION: 151] Care to be taken by bailee — In all cases of bailment, the bailee must take as much care of the goods bailed as a man of ordinary prudence would take of his own goods of similar quality and value.

[SECTION: 152] Bailee when not liable — If the bailee has taken the amount of care required by Section 151, he is not responsible for the loss, destruction or deterioration of the thing bailed.

[SECTION: 154] Liability for unauthorized use — If the bailee makes any unauthorized use of the goods bailed, he is liable to make compensation to the bailor for any damage arising from such use.

[SECTION: 172] Pledge defined — The bailment of goods as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise is called pledge. The bailor is called the pawnor and the bailee is called the pawnee.

[SECTION: 173] Pawnee's right of retainer — The pawnee may retain the goods pledged as security for the payment of the debt or the performance of the promise.

[SECTION: 176] Pawnee's right where pawnor defaults — If the pawnor makes default in payment of the debt or performance of the promise, the pawnee may sell the goods pledged after giving reasonable notice to the pawnor.

[SECTION: 182] Agent defined — An agent is a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom such act is done or who is so represented is called the principal.

[SECTION: 183] Who may employ agent — Any person who is of the age of majority and of sound mind may employ an agent.

[SECTION: 184] Who may be agent — As between the principal and third persons, any person may become an agent.

[SECTION: 185] No consideration for agency — No consideration is necessary to create an agency.

[SECTION: 186] Agent's authority — The authority of an agent may be express or implied.

[SECTION: 187] Implied authority — Authority is said to be implied when it is inferred from the circumstances of the case, and things spoken or written, or the ordinary course of dealing.

[SECTION: 188] Extent of agent's authority — An agent having authority to do an act has authority to do every lawful thing which is necessary in order to do such act.

[SECTION: 189] Agency for sale — An agent having authority to carry on a business has authority to do all things usual in the course of such business.

[SECTION: 196] Ratification — Where acts are done by one person on behalf of another but without his knowledge or authority, the other may elect to ratify such acts. The effect of ratification is the same as if the acts had been performed by authority.

[SECTION: 201] Termination of agency — An agency is terminated: by the principal revoking the authority; by the agent renouncing the business; by completion of business; by death or insanity of principal or agent; by insolvency of principal.

[SECTION: 211] Agent's duty — An agent must conduct the business of his principal according to the directions given by the principal. In the absence of directions, according to the custom which prevails in doing business of the same kind.

[SECTION: 212] Skill and diligence — An agent must act with reasonable diligence and use such skill as he possesses.

[SECTION: 214] Agent's duty to communicate — An agent must communicate with his principal and take instructions from him in cases of difficulty.

[SECTION: 222] Principal's liability for agent's acts — The employer of an agent is bound to indemnify him against the consequences of all lawful acts done by the agent in exercise of his authority.

[SECTION: 226] Principal bound by agent's acts — Contracts entered into through an agent and obligations arising from acts done by an agent in the exercise of his authority have the same legal consequences as if the contracts had been entered into and the acts done by the principal personally.

[SECTION: 229] Notice to agent — Any notice given to or information obtained by the agent in the course of business conducted by him for the principal shall, as between the principal and third parties, have the same legal consequences as if it had been given to or obtained by the principal.
